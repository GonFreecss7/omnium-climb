Tags indicate roughly when a technique is worth learning:

- `core` — learn first. Pays off immediately, little injury risk.
- `later` — useful, once the core set is solid.
- `advanced` — worth knowing by name; relevant at higher grades or in competition.
- `caution` — meaningful injury risk, especially for fingers and elbows. Introduce late, never while something already hurts.

`nonstandard: true` marks a name that is not standardised across gyms. The move is real; the label may differ.

### 7. Centre-of-Gravity Techniques

These all do the same job: move body mass over the feet so the arms stop holding you on. For most climbers this is the highest-value category to work on.

#### Back step
- id: `back-step`
- tag: `core`
- what: One foot on its outside edge with the hip turned into the wall, so your body goes side-on instead of square.
- how: Place the little-toe edge on the hold, turn that hip toward the wall, let the opposite shoulder drop back. Your reaching arm now comes from a turned torso, not a stretch.
- best: Long reaches, steep walls, and saving your arms. Learn this before anything else on this list.

#### Drop knee
- id: `drop-knee`
- tag: `later`
- what: A backstep taken further — the knee rotates downward and you actively press outward through the foot.
- how: From a backstep with feet reasonably wide, rotate the knee down and in, pressing the foot into the hold. The pressure locks you in and pulls your hips to the wall.
- best: Steep terrain and long reaches. Very hard on the knee joint (meniscus), so use sparingly and never with a violent rotation.

#### Outside flag
- id: `outside-flag`
- tag: `core`
- what: The free leg extends out to the same side it belongs to, acting as a counterweight.
- how: Standing on one foot, swing the other leg out to its own side and press the toe lightly against the wall. Adjust how far out until the swing stops.
- best: Stopping barn-doors when the free foot has nothing to stand on. The simplest flag — start here.

#### Back flag
- id: `back-flag`
- tag: `core`
- what: The free leg crosses behind the standing leg and presses the wall.
- how: Standing on the left foot, sweep the right leg behind the left and smear the toe on the wall to the left. Your hips turn side-on automatically.
- best: The most useful flag in the gym. Fixes rotation on side-pulls and lets you reach far with one arm.

#### Inside flag
- id: `inside-flag`
- tag: `later`
- what: The free leg crosses in front of the standing leg.
- how: Standing on one foot, bring the other leg across the front and press the toe on the wall or a foothold on the far side.
- best: Tight, balancy positions where there's no room to swing the leg behind. Less common than the back flag.

#### Arm flag
- id: `arm-flag`
- tag: `later`
- what: The same counterweight idea, but with a free arm instead of a leg.
- how: Extend the non-holding arm out and away from the wall to shift your mass sideways. Sometimes you just push the wall lightly with the palm.
- best: Slab, delicate balance, and moments where both feet are already committed.

#### Perching
- id: `perching`
- tag: `core`
- what: Standing tall and balanced directly over one high foot, hips stacked above the toe, arms barely loaded.
- how: Get the foot high, shift your hips forward and up over it, then stand up through the leg. Resist pulling with the arms — they're only for balance.
- best: Slab, vertical walls, volumes, and rests. The cheapest way to gain height that exists.

#### Frogging
- id: `frogging`
- tag: `core`
- what: Both knees turned out and dropped wide, hips pressed close to the wall.
- how: Turn both feet outward on their inside edges, let the knees splay, and push the pelvis into the wall.
- best: Bringing hips close on vertical and slightly overhanging walls, and for resting. Also a good hip-mobility indicator.

#### Inside figure 4
- id: `inside-figure-4`
- tag: `advanced`
- what: You hook a leg over your own arm, on the inside, and sit on it to gain height without footholds.
- how: Hang from one arm, bring the leg on the same side over that forearm, then press down through the leg to push your body up.
- best: Overhangs with no feet — mostly a comp and steep-cave move. High elbow and shoulder load; not a beginner move.

#### Outside figure 4
- id: `outside-figure-4`
- tag: `advanced`
- what: Same idea, but the leg hooks over the arm from the outside.
- how: Hang from one arm, swing the opposite leg over that arm from outside, and press up.
- best: Rare, awkward, high-injury-risk. Filed under 'know the name, don't practise it'.

#### The Janja
- id: `the-janja`
- tag: `advanced`
- nonstandard: true
- what: Gym slang for the extreme hip-close, deep-turn body position associated with Janja Garnbret's style — usually a very compressed drop-knee-like position where the hips stay glued to the wall through a long move.
- how: Essentially a maximal version of the backstep/drop knee with a lot of hip flexibility and core tension.
- best: Nothing you need yet. The name isn't standardised — check how your gym uses it, because different walls use it for different moves.

#### Peter Pan
- id: `peter-pan`
- tag: `advanced`
- nonstandard: true
- what: A dynamic move where you launch into a fully extended, briefly airborne position — arms and legs stretched out — before catching the target hold.
- how: Generate from the legs, extend the whole body in flight, and catch the hold at full stretch.
- best: Steep, powerful comp-style problems. Name varies by gym; high impact, so not a beginner priority.

### 8. Foot Techniques

Everything here is about turning a foothold into something that can bear weight or pull you into the wall. Feet are where a beginner has the most unclaimed progress.

#### Inside edge
- id: `inside-edge`
- tag: `core`
- what: Standing on the big-toe side of the shoe.
- how: Place the front-inside point of the shoe on the hold and drop the heel. Keeps you square to the wall.
- best: Frontal climbing, slab, and any move where you want stability over reach.

#### Outside edge
- id: `outside-edge`
- tag: `core`
- what: Standing on the little-toe side of the shoe.
- how: Roll onto the outer front point and turn the hip in. This is what makes a backstep possible.
- best: Side-on positions, long reaches, and steep walls.

#### Smear
- id: `smear`
- tag: `core`
- what: Using friction against the flat wall or a sloping surface with no defined hold.
- how: Press the ball of the foot flat against the wall, keep the heel low, and push through it. Trust it — hesitation reduces surface contact.
- best: Slab, volumes, and filling gaps where footholds don't exist.

#### Alpine knee
- id: `alpine-knee`
- tag: `caution`
- what: Putting the knee directly on a hold or ledge instead of the foot.
- how: Kneel onto the hold to gain height, then bring the foot up.
- best: Occasionally the only option outdoors or on a mantle. Frowned upon in the gym, it strips skin, and it puts your kneecap on a hard edge. Learn the rock-over instead.

#### Heel hook
- id: `heel-hook`
- tag: `core`
- what: Placing the heel on a hold and pulling with the hamstring, effectively using the leg as a third arm.
- how: Set the heel on the hold, point the toe slightly outward, then actively pull — think of dragging the hold toward your hips. Keep tension; a loose heel slips.
- best: Steep ground, topping out, and taking weight off the arms. Warm your hamstrings up first — cold heel hooks strain them.

#### Toe hook
- id: `toe-hook`
- tag: `later`
- what: Hooking the top of the foot under or against a hold to stop your body swinging out.
- how: Press the top of the toes against the underside or far side of a hold and pull with the shin and hamstring.
- best: Roofs, steep compression, and stopping a cut-loose from swinging you off.

#### Bat hang
- id: `bat-hang`
- tag: `advanced`
- what: Hanging upside down from both feet, hands free.
- how: Hook both toes over a solid feature (usually a roof lip or big volume) and let go with the hands.
- best: Roof problems and comp gimmicks. Fun, but a bad fall position — only with pads and supervision.

#### Bicycle
- id: `bicycle`
- tag: `later`
- what: One foot pushes on a hold while the other toe-hooks the opposite side of the same hold — the two feet squeeze it.
- how: Press with one toe, hook with the other, and clamp. The hold is now held between your feet.
- best: Steep terrain and roofs where you need your feet to stay on while your hands move.

#### Heel-toe cam
- id: `heel-toe-cam`
- tag: `later`
- what: Wedging the foot between two surfaces, heel on one and toe on the other, so the foot locks in place.
- how: Find a slot, corner, or gap between volumes; set the heel on one side and jam the toes against the other until it's snug.
- best: No-hands rests, roofs, and holding position while you shake out. Powerful, and it can trap your ankle — be able to release it.

#### Frog pinch
- id: `frog-pinch`
- tag: `advanced`
- nonstandard: true
- what: Squeezing a volume or large feature between both feet, knees splayed like a frog.
- how: Place both feet on either side of the feature and press inward with the legs.
- best: Comp-style volume climbing. Name varies by gym.

#### Knee bar
- id: `knee-bar`
- tag: `later`
- what: Wedging the leg between two surfaces — knee/thigh pressing one, foot pressing the other — so the leg locks solid.
- how: Slot the foot on one hold, then drive the top of the knee or thigh against the opposing surface until the pressure holds you.
- best: The best rest in climbing when you find one. Often lets you take both hands off. Worth actively hunting for on roofs.

### 9. Hand & Grip Techniques

Grip choice is where most finger and elbow injuries are made. Everything below is real technique, but the order you learn them in matters — the strongest grips are also the most stressful.

#### Open hand / three-finger drag
- id: `open-hand-three-finger-drag`
- tag: `core`
- what: Fingers almost straight, hanging from the last two joints, thumb not involved. Usually index, middle and ring on the hold.
- how: Drape the fingers over the hold and let them extend. It should feel like hanging, not gripping.
- best: The safest and most sustainable grip. Best on slopers, big edges and jugs, and the sensible default for anyone with irritable fingers or elbows.

#### Open crimp
- id: `open-crimp`
- tag: `core`
- what: A slightly closed open hand — fingers curved but the last joint isn't hyperextended.
- how: Curl the fingers gently onto the edge without engaging the thumb.
- best: General-purpose grip on small edges. A good middle ground between drag and half crimp.

#### Half crimp
- id: `half-crimp`
- tag: `core`
- what: Middle knuckles bent to roughly 90 degrees, fingertip joint flat, thumb off.
- how: Set the fingers on the edge, bend the big knuckles to a right angle, and keep the fingertips flat rather than curling.
- best: The most trainable and transferable grip position. This should be your main strength position long-term.

#### Quarter crimp
- id: `quarter-crimp`
- tag: `later`
- what: Between drag and half crimp — big knuckles bent slightly, fingertip joints still flat.
- how: Half crimp shape with less bend at the knuckle.
- best: Small, positive edges where full half-crimp feels cramped. Common in modern finger training.

#### Closed crimp / full crimp
- id: `closed-crimp-full-crimp`
- tag: `caution`
- what: Fingertip joints hyperextended and the thumb wrapped over the index finger. 'Closed crimp' and 'full crimp' are used interchangeably by most climbers.
- how: Curl the fingers hard onto a small edge, then lock the thumb over the index fingernail.
- best: Maximum force on tiny edges, and maximum stress on the A2 pulley and the medial elbow. The most common source of pulley injuries — introduce it late, and use it sparingly.

#### Two-finger pocket
- id: `two-finger-pocket`
- tag: `later`
- what: Only two fingers fit the hold.
- how: Usually middle + ring (strongest pairing) or index + middle. Keep the wrist neutral and don't twist.
- best: Pocketed holds. Load builds fast on a small area — introduce gradually.

#### Front three
- id: `front-three`
- tag: `later`
- what: Index, middle and ring fingers on the hold; pinky off.
- how: The natural three-finger position for most people.
- best: Medium pockets and edges too small for four fingers.

#### Back three
- id: `back-three`
- tag: `later`
- what: Middle, ring and pinky on the hold; index off.
- how: Slide the index off the edge and load the outer three.
- best: Awkward hold orientations and, for some people, a stronger position than front three. Worth testing which of the two is stronger.

#### Mono
- id: `mono`
- tag: `caution`
- what: A single finger in a pocket.
- how: Usually the middle finger. Keep the arm aligned under the pocket and avoid any twisting load.
- best: Rare in gyms, brutal on tendons. Not something a beginner should seek out.

#### Closed mono
- id: `closed-mono`
- tag: `caution`
- what: A mono with the thumb locked over the finger for extra force.
- how: As above, with the thumb wrapping the finger.
- best: Very high injury risk for very little gain below advanced grades. Know the name; skip the practice.

#### Stacked fingers
- id: `stacked-fingers`
- tag: `advanced`
- what: Stacking one finger on top of another in a pocket too small for two side by side.
- how: Place the middle finger in the pocket, then rest the index on top of it to add force.
- best: Small pockets, mostly outdoors. Uncommon in gyms.

#### Pinch
- id: `pinch`
- tag: `core`
- what: Squeezing a hold between the fingers and the thumb.
- how: Set the fingers on one side, thumb on the other, and squeeze. Keep the wrist stiff so the force goes into the hold.
- best: Very common in modern setting. Trains thumb strength and is relatively kind to the fingers.

#### Thumbercling / thumb catch
- id: `thumbercling-thumb-catch`
- tag: `later`
- what: Using the thumb on the underside or edge of a hold to add direction or stability.
- how: Hook the thumb pad under a lip or against a corner of the hold while the fingers work from above.
- best: Slopers and volumes where the thumb turns a bad hold into a usable one.

#### Palm down / palming
- id: `palm-down-palming`
- tag: `core`
- what: Pressing with the flat of the palm rather than gripping anything.
- how: Place the palm flat on a volume or the wall and push downward or outward.
- best: Slab, mantles, stemming and rests. Zero finger load, so it is always worth looking for.

#### Meat hook
- id: `meat-hook`
- tag: `advanced`
- nonstandard: true
- what: Using the fleshy edge of the hand, wrist or forearm hooked over a feature instead of a normal grip.
- how: Drape the side of the hand or forearm over the hold and pull with the arm rather than the fingers.
- best: Big volumes and rounded features. Name varies by gym.

### 10. Positions & Hold Orientations

The same hold can be four different holds depending on where your body is. This category is really about reading direction of pull.

#### Side pull
- id: `side-pull`
- tag: `core`
- what: A vertically-oriented hold pulled sideways, toward your midline.
- how: Lean your body away from the hold so your weight opposes it, and keep the arm relatively straight.
- best: Extremely common. The key insight: a side pull only works if your body counterbalances it.

#### Lay back
- id: `lay-back`
- tag: `core`
- what: Pulling on a side pull or edge while pushing with the feet in the opposite direction, body leaning off to the side.
- how: Hands pull one way, feet press the other, and the opposition holds you on. Keep arms straight and walk the feet up.
- best: Aretes, corners, and flakes. Efficient when done with straight arms; exhausting when done with bent ones.

#### Gaston
- id: `gaston`
- tag: `later`
- what: A side pull pulled outward, away from your midline, thumb down and elbow out — like prying open a lift door.
- how: Thumb pointing down, elbow high and out, and push/pull outward while your feet push you into the wall.
- best: When the hold faces the wrong way. Shoulder-intensive — keep the elbow from flaring beyond comfort.

#### Elevator door
- id: `elevator-door`
- tag: `later`
- nonstandard: true
- what: Two opposing gastons at the same time — both hands pressing outward, like forcing lift doors apart.
- how: One hand on each hold, elbows out, press outward on both simultaneously and keep body tension.
- best: Symmetrical comp-style sequences. Heavy on the shoulders.

#### Undercling
- id: `undercling`
- tag: `core`
- what: A hold gripped from underneath, palm facing up.
- how: Get your feet high so your hips rise above the hold — an undercling only becomes useful once you're above it. Pull up and in.
- best: Under roofs and flakes. High feet are the whole trick.

#### Compression
- id: `compression`
- tag: `later`
- what: Squeezing two opposing holds or features toward each other to stay on.
- how: Hands (and often feet) press inward from both sides. Body tension holds the whole thing together.
- best: Volumes, aretes and prows. Very common in modern setting.

#### Inverted
- id: `inverted`
- tag: `advanced`
- what: Any position where your hips are above your shoulders — upside down or nearly so.
- how: Usually reached via a toe hook or bat hang, with heavy core engagement.
- best: Roofs. Disorienting, so learn it low with pads.

#### Outward facing
- id: `outward-facing`
- tag: `later`
- what: Climbing with your back or side to the wall rather than your chest.
- how: Turn out and use the wall behind you for pressure, often with a heel hook or stem.
- best: Corners, chimneys, and awkward comp volumes.

#### Gastondercling
- id: `gastondercling`
- tag: `advanced`
- nonstandard: true
- what: A hybrid where a hold is used as a gaston and an undercling at once — elbow out, palm rotated up.
- how: Thumb-down gaston grip while the pull direction is upward from underneath.
- best: Awkward hold orientations. Slang, and not universal.

#### Lock off
- id: `lock-off`
- tag: `later`
- what: Holding a static bent-arm position so the other hand can reach.
- how: Pull the hold to chest or shoulder height, engage the shoulder, and hold still while the other arm moves.
- best: Precise reaches to bad holds. Note: this is the single most elbow-loading position in climbing — prefer deadpointing when elbows are irritable.

#### Stemming / bridging
- id: `stemming-bridging`
- tag: `core`
- what: Pressing outward with both feet (and sometimes hands) against two opposing surfaces.
- how: Feet wide on either side of a corner or between two volumes, push outward, and stand tall.
- best: Corners and dihedrals. Often a full no-hands rest — worth spotting.

#### The chin
- id: `the-chin`
- tag: `advanced`
- nonstandard: true
- what: Using the chin, head or shoulder to press against a hold or volume for a moment of extra stability.
- how: Press the chin or side of the head onto the hold while both hands reposition.
- best: Almost entirely a comp trick. Real, occasionally decisive, and mildly ridiculous.

### 11. Movement Techniques

How you get from one position to the next. Most of these are cheap to learn and immediately useful.

#### Hand match
- id: `hand-match`
- tag: `core`
- what: Both hands on the same hold.
- how: Make room by shifting the first hand to one side, then bring the second alongside. On small holds, move fingers one at a time.
- best: Changing lead hand. Fundamental.

#### Piano match
- id: `piano-match`
- tag: `later`
- what: Matching by walking the fingers across a hold one at a time, like piano keys.
- how: Lift and replace individual fingers of the first hand while the second hand's fingers take their place.
- best: Small edges where there's no room for a normal match.

#### Foot match
- id: `foot-match`
- tag: `core`
- what: Both feet on the same hold, or swapping which foot is on it.
- how: Either share the hold, or hop-swap: quick, quiet, controlled. Keep hands on and hips close during the swap.
- best: Changing which side of your body leads. Extremely common.

#### Hand-foot match
- id: `hand-foot-match`
- tag: `later`
- what: A hand and a foot on the same hold at the same time.
- how: Grip the hold, then bring the toe onto the same hold beside your fingers — usually to the outside of the hand.
- best: Roofs, mantles, and steep problems with sparse feet. Watch your fingers.

#### Hand cross (over)
- id: `hand-cross-over`
- tag: `core`
- what: Reaching one hand over the other to a hold on the far side.
- how: Turn the hips side-on first, then cross the arm over the top. Crossing without turning the hips is what makes it feel impossible.
- best: Traverses and diagonal sequences. Saves an intermediate move.

#### Hand cross (under)
- id: `hand-cross-under`
- tag: `later`
- what: Crossing one arm underneath the other.
- how: Drop the shoulder and pass the arm below. Often more stable than crossing over.
- best: Undercling sequences and low traverses.

#### Rose move
- id: `rose-move`
- tag: `advanced`
- what: A dramatic cross where the reaching arm passes behind your own head to a hold on the far side.
- how: Turn fully side-on, drop the shoulder, and reach back and across, usually to a gaston or side pull.
- best: Rare, showy, and genuinely useful on some diagonal sequences. Shoulder-intensive.

#### Foot cross (front)
- id: `foot-cross-front`
- tag: `later`
- what: Crossing one foot in front of the other to reach a hold on the far side.
- how: Keep hips close, cross the free foot in front, and place precisely without bumping the standing leg.
- best: Traverses. Often better than a series of small foot shuffles.

#### Foot cross (back)
- id: `foot-cross-back`
- tag: `later`
- what: Crossing one foot behind the other.
- how: Sweep the foot behind the standing leg and place it. Naturally turns you side-on.
- best: Traverses where crossing in front would push your hips off the wall.

#### Rock over
- id: `rock-over`
- tag: `core`
- what: Shifting your centre of gravity from below a high foot to above it, then standing up on it.
- how: Get the foot high, push your hips forward and over the toe, keep the shoulders coming with you, then press through the leg.
- best: Slab and vertical walls. This is the move that converts leg strength into height, and a high priority for any beginner.

#### Knee press
- id: `knee-press`
- tag: `later`
- what: Pressing the knee or shin into the wall or a volume for stability.
- how: Drive the knee into the surface to create friction and stop rotation while a hand moves.
- best: Volumes and slab. A cheap stabiliser.

#### Mantle
- id: `mantle`
- tag: `core`
- what: Pushing down on a hold or ledge to get your body above it, like climbing out of a swimming pool.
- how: Pull to chest height, rotate the hand so the palm presses down, press until the arm locks, then bring a foot up.
- best: Topping out. Essential and often the crux of a problem for beginners.

#### Beached whale
- id: `beached-whale`
- tag: `later`
- what: The inelegant version of a mantle — flopping the torso onto the ledge and wriggling up.
- how: Belly on the ledge, then squirm.
- best: Getting up when the clean mantle fails. It counts. It just doesn't look good.

#### Cut loose
- id: `cut-loose`
- tag: `later`
- what: Letting both feet come off the wall so your body swings, then re-engaging them.
- how: Accept the swing, keep the core tight, control the return, and put the feet back deliberately rather than scrabbling.
- best: Steep ground where feet can't follow the hands. Often faster than fighting to keep them on.

#### Campussing
- id: `campussing`
- tag: `caution`
- what: Climbing with no feet at all, hand over hand.
- how: Pull between holds using arms only.
- best: Sometimes the intended sequence on steep problems. Very high elbow and shoulder load, and a leading cause of climber's elbow — avoid until well adapted.

#### The Houdini
- id: `the-houdini`
- tag: `advanced`
- nonstandard: true
- what: Escaping a tangled or reversed body position by rotating through it rather than reversing the moves.
- how: Usually a hip rotation under an arm to unwind crossed limbs.
- best: Getting out of a sequence you read wrong. Name varies by gym.

#### Good Samaritan
- id: `good-samaritan`
- tag: `later`
- nonstandard: true
- what: Using a hold or feature that isn't part of your problem — or one hand helping the other — to save a position.
- how: Grab whatever is available to stabilise, usually mid-fall or mid-mistake.
- best: Practically: a bail-out. Off-route holds mean the send doesn't count. Name varies by gym.

#### T-Rex turtle roll
- id: `t-rex-turtle-roll`
- tag: `advanced`
- nonstandard: true
- what: Gym slang for rolling the body over a lip or volume with arms tucked close, when there's nothing to push on.
- how: Commit the hips over the feature and roll, keeping the arms in tight.
- best: Awkward topouts on rounded features. Definitely slang — expect your gym to have its own name.

### 12. Dynamic Techniques

Powerful and fun, and the highest-impact category for tendons and joints. Ability here usually arrives before the tissue is ready for it, so restraint is the skill to build.

#### Dead point
- id: `dead-point`
- tag: `core`
- what: Catching the hold at the weightless instant at the top of an upward move.
- how: Generate from the legs, and time the hand to arrive exactly when your upward momentum stops. Not before, not after.
- best: Almost every reach. Cheaper than a lock-off and far kinder to the elbows. The most important item in this section.

#### Dyno
- id: `dyno`
- tag: `later`
- what: A jump where all four points leave the wall.
- how: Load the legs by dropping into a coil, drive upward through the feet, and keep your eyes on the target hold the whole time.
- best: Moves that are simply too far to reach. Fun, high-impact — a couple per session, not a training focus.

#### Double dyno
- id: `double-dyno`
- tag: `later`
- what: A dyno caught with both hands simultaneously.
- how: As above, but keep the body square so both hands arrive together.
- best: Big jugs and comp-style jumps. Landing one is a power indicator, not a reason to build a session around them.

#### One-arm dyno
- id: `one-arm-dyno`
- tag: `advanced`
- what: A dyno caught on a single hand.
- how: Generate from the legs and lead with the catching arm; the other arm stays back for balance.
- best: Comp problems. Very high shoulder and elbow impact.

#### Double clutch
- id: `double-clutch`
- tag: `advanced`
- what: A dyno where both hands leave, one hand catches briefly, and the other immediately follows or bumps onward.
- how: Catch and stabilise with the first hand while the second arrives in the same motion.
- best: Steep sequences with no intermediate holds. Advanced.

#### Paddle dyno (triple / quadruple)
- id: `paddle-dyno-triple-quadruple`
- tag: `advanced`
- what: A dyno where a hand slaps one or more intermediate holds mid-flight to redirect momentum before the real catch.
- how: Use each intermediate slap to push or steer rather than to hold.
- best: Comp setting. Spectacular, rarely necessary.

#### Step-up dyno
- id: `step-up-dyno`
- tag: `later`
- what: A dyno generated by stepping up onto a high foot and springing from it, rather than coiling both legs.
- how: Place the high foot, load it, and drive upward through that single leg.
- best: When the feet are too high or awkward for a two-legged coil.

#### Hip swing
- id: `hip-swing`
- tag: `later`
- what: Generating momentum by swinging the hips rather than pulling with the arms.
- how: Initiate the swing from the core and time the hand release to the peak.
- best: Steep ground. This is how strong climbers make hard moves look easy.

#### Arm swing
- id: `arm-swing`
- tag: `later`
- what: Using the free arm's swing to add momentum to a move.
- how: Swing the free arm upward in time with the leg drive.
- best: Adds a surprising amount to a dyno or long reach.

#### Pogo / moon kick
- id: `pogo-moon-kick`
- tag: `advanced`
- what: Kicking one leg out and down to generate momentum, like a pogo stick, when there are no useful footholds.
- how: Swing the free leg down and back hard, and use the reaction to drive your body up toward the hold.
- best: Steep problems with one foothold and a long move. Looks strange, works well.

#### Run and jump
- id: `run-and-jump`
- tag: `advanced`
- what: A dyno started from the ground with running steps.
- how: Two or three steps into the wall, then jump for the start or first hold.
- best: Comp-style jump starts. Uncontrolled landings — pads and spotters.

#### Swing and jump
- id: `swing-and-jump`
- tag: `advanced`
- what: Building a pendulum swing on the holds, then releasing at the peak into a jump.
- how: Swing the body once or twice to build amplitude, release at the top of the arc.
- best: Roofs and comp problems where a static jump can't reach.

#### Headbutt
- id: `headbutt`
- tag: `advanced`
- nonstandard: true
- what: Using the head or forehead pressed against a volume for a moment of stability, usually during compression.
- how: Press the head into the surface to add a third contact point while hands reposition.
- best: Rare comp trick. Real, but not something to seek out.
